How long does stain last on wood siding?

Every painter has a rule of thumb. The federal Forest Products Laboratory has actual numbers, by finish, wood and surface texture. Here they are, what they mean for a house in Boulder County, and a calculator for when your walls are due.

  • Semitransparent stain, rough-sawn cedar: 4 to 8 years
  • Solid-color stain, rough-sawn cedar: 10 to 15 years
  • Paint, primer plus two coats, rough-sawn cedar: 15 to 20 years
  • Smooth wood: roughly half the stain life

By Boulder CO Painters · Published October 22, 2026

Direct answer: According to the USDA Forest Products Laboratory, semitransparent stain lasts 4 to 8 years on rough-sawn cedar siding and about 2 to 4 on smooth cedar. Solid-color stain lasts 10 to 15 years on rough-sawn cedar, and paint with a primer and two topcoats 15 to 20. Those are national averages; sunny Boulder walls usually come due sooner.

What does the federal data actually say?

The Wood Handbook, published by the USDA Forest Service’s Forest Products Laboratory in Madison, Wisconsin, is the standard federal reference on wood. Chapter 16 covers finishes, and its Table 16-2 lists the expected service life of each type of finish on each type of siding. The handbook says the estimates were compiled from observations of many researchers, apply to an average location in the contiguous United States, and depend on climate and exposure.

The PDF’s table layout is hard to read; we matched each row to the handbook’s footnotes and text, which say stain on smooth wood lasts about half as long as on saw-textured wood, and that primer plus two latex topcoats on saw-textured wood can reach 20 years.

Why does rough-sawn siding hold a finish longer?

Because it holds more finish. A rough, saw-textured surface soaks up more stain and carries a thicker paint film than a planed, smooth board. The handbook puts it plainly for stains: used on smooth wood, expect approximately half the service life you would get on a saw-textured surface. It also notes that saw-textured or weathered surfaces can take two coats of stain, with the second applied while the first is still wet.

The species matters too. The handbook notes that wide, dense latewood bands, typical of Douglas-fir and southern yellow pine, often lead to early paint failure, while western redcedar, redwood and white pine have thin latewood bands and give excellent paint performance. That is part of why cedar rows in the table score higher than pine and fir, and why smooth flat-grain boards, which show those bands broadly across the face, score below vertical-grain ones.

Why does paint last longer, and why does that not settle it?

A film of paint blocks sunlight and water more completely than a penetrating stain. That is why its numbers are higher. But a film can fail by cracking and peeling, while a penetrating stain simply wears away. The handbook makes the trade-off clear in Table 16-3: a sound semitransparent stain is maintained by cleaning and applying another coat, while paint that has peeled has to be scraped, the bare wood primed and new topcoats applied.

So the useful comparison is not just years between coats but what each coat costs. Stain comes due more often, but each coat is lighter work. Paint comes due less often, but if it is left until it peels, the next job is bigger.

What shortens the life of a finish in Boulder County?

  • More ultraviolet. The EPA says UV rises about 6% per kilometer of elevation. NOAA lists the Boulder weather station at 1,671.5 meters, roughly 10% more UV than at sea level (our arithmetic). Houses up the canyons sit higher still.
  • Freeze-thaw. The Boulder station averages 138.2 nights a year at or below freezing (NOAA 1991–2020 normals). Every night that freezes water in a crack or a checked board opens it a little more.
  • Which way the wall faces. South and west walls take the most sun and come due first; north walls under deep eaves can go much longer. Most houses are not due all at once.
  • Too few coats. For paint, the handbook says a primer plus one topcoat lasts about half as long as primer plus two.
  • Bare time before the first coat. In Forest Products Laboratory tests, cedar left in the sun 16 weeks before painting needed refinishing within 3 years; boards exposed for 1 week lasted 13 years before peeling.
  • Water from the wrong places. Sprinklers on the siding, gutters that overflow and plants against the wall keep wood wet. The handbook is clear that paint is not a preservative and will not prevent decay if the wood stays moist.

The 30-second water test

Flick water onto the sunniest wall. If it beads, the stain is still working. If it soaks in and darkens the wood right away, the water repellency is gone and the wall is due.

How do you read your own walls?

The table gives a range. Your house tells you where in that range it is. Walk around it on a bright morning with a spray bottle and look at each wall separately:

  • Color. Compare the sunniest wall with the most sheltered one, such as the area under a deep porch roof. If the sunny wall is several shades lighter, the pigment that protects the wood is wearing off.
  • Water. Spray a few boards. Beading means the finish is still repelling water; quick soaking and darkening means it is not. Test low on the wall too, where splash and snow sit.
  • Texture. Run a hand across the boards. A smooth, even feel is good. Fuzzy, raised fibers or a gray powder on your hand mean the wood surface itself has started to weather.
  • Film edges. On solid stain or paint, look at board ends, the bottom edges of laps and around windows. Cracks and lifting along those edges come before peeling across the face.
  • Dark spots. Black or green speckling is usually mildew. It can be washed off, but the handbook treats it as a sign the finish is due.
  • Soft wood. Press a screwdriver into the lowest boards and window sills. Soft wood is a repair, and no finish fixes it.

Write down which walls fail which tests. A house where only the south and west walls fail is a partial job, and a much smaller bill, than a house where every wall does.

How do you make a finish last longer?

  1. Recoat before it fails. A stain that still has color and only lost its water beading needs a cleaning and a coat. Wait until the wood is gray and fuzzy, and it needs cleaning, brightening and sanding first.
  2. Do the sunny walls on their own schedule. There is no rule that the whole house has to be done at once. South and west elevations between full repaints is a common, sensible plan.
  3. Use enough finish. On rough-sawn wood, two coats of stain applied wet-on-wet, and for paint, a primer plus two topcoats.
  4. Choose more pigment. The handbook notes that the higher the pigment concentration, the longer a tinted finish lasts, and that almost all pigments block UV.
  5. Keep water off the wall. Redirect sprinklers, clean gutters, and keep soil and plants back from the bottom boards.
  6. Wash off mildew. The handbook notes that mildew growth on the surface usually indicates the need for refinishing; cleaning it promptly helps the next coat bond.

What does refinishing cost when it comes due?

There is no Boulder-specific Angi page, so we use its Denver-area exterior guide, which lists wood siding at $1 to $3.05 per square foot and paint stripping at $0.50 to $2.05 per square foot when an old film has failed. That stripping line is the cost of waiting too long on a film finish. Our Boulder painting cost page has the rest of the price picture, and the cedar staining page has an estimator for your walls.

Timing matters too. At the Boulder station the median last spring freeze is May 6 and the median first fall freeze is October 5, so a wall that comes due in August may have to wait for the following May if it is not booked. Questions

Siding finish questions

How long does stain last on cedar siding?
The USDA Wood Handbook lists semitransparent stain at 4 to 8 years on rough-sawn cedar and 2 to 4 years on smooth cedar, and solid-color stain at 10 to 15 years on rough-sawn cedar. Those are averages for the contiguous U.S.; sun-facing walls at Boulder’s altitude usually come due first.
How long does exterior paint last on wood siding?
With a primer and two topcoats, the handbook lists 15 to 20 years on rough-sawn cedar and 10 to 15 years on smooth vertical-grain cedar. With only one topcoat over the primer, expect about half of that.
How do I know when my stain needs another coat?
Sprinkle water on the sunniest wall. If it soaks in quickly instead of beading, the stain has lost its water repellency. Graying, mildew and color that has faded to bare wood in spots are the other signs.
Is it cheaper to restain before the old stain fails?
Usually. A sound penetrating stain can be cleaned and recoated. Once the wood has weathered gray, it needs cleaning, brightening and sanding first, and a failed film has to be stripped, which Angi’s Denver-area guide prices at $0.50 to $2.05 per square foot on top of the coating.
Does a darker stain last longer?
Generally, more pigment means more protection. The handbook notes that the higher the pigment concentration of a tinted finish, the longer its service life, and that almost all pigments block ultraviolet light.
